## Findings

- Ranking (oracle NDCG@3, test): hybrid 0.548; rules 0.417, popularity 0.391, v1 forest 0.369, random 0.254; hidden-truth ceiling 1.000. The model recovers part of the structure we wrote into the generator, and much is left.
- The hybrid ranks only **marginally** better than its best component alone (factorization 0.546 vs hybrid 0.548, components trained on the same people; all: content 0.544, collaborative 0.434, factorization 0.546). The case for stacking rests on the calibrated probabilities that suggestion thresholds and evidence levels need, not on ranking.
- Suggestions actually offered: the hybrid offers something to 9.6% of test people, and 12.8% of the items it offers would not help (true benefit ≤ 0); the rules offer to 37.4% with 48.1% not helping. Because the rules reach far more people, their expected benefit per person overall (0.1607) is higher than the hybrid's (0.0692): the hybrid is right far more often but helps fewer people in total. That is the reach / wrong-suggestion trade-off in §10.
- Calibration on held-out logged feedback: ECE 0.013. But the 0.7-0.8 bin (n=100) predicts 0.736 against an observed 0.610; the 0.8-0.9 bin (n=35) predicts 0.835 against an observed 0.771: the highest probabilities are overconfident, and that is where suggestion thresholds sit.
- Cold start: NDCG@3 ranges from 0.477 (cold: 0 answers) to 0.563 (cold: 5+ answers).
- Groups: the lowest worst/best NDCG@3 ratio within a family is 0.903 (new to credit: no vs new to credit: yes); the age-band ratio is 0.929. These are synthetic groups (see FAIRNESS.md).
- Overfitting: hybrid AUC train 0.753 / validation 0.722 / test 0.720; NDCG@3 0.541 / 0.553 / 0.548.

How to read this: the **oracle** numbers judge each system's ranking of every eligible item against each held-out synthetic person's hidden truth (relevant = would keep it with probability > 0.5 *and* it truly helps; NDCG gain = max(0, true benefit)). The **logged** numbers use only what the biased logging policy happened to show, as real feedback would. "False rec" = recommended items whose true benefit is ≤ 0. "Kept but no help" = share of expected keeps that are items which do not help (the gap between clicking Keep and being helped).

## 10. Model selection: the precision target (validation people only)

The hybrid was trained once per precision target (engine `minPrecision` ∈ {0.6, 0.65, 0.7, 0.75}; ranking is identical, only the per-item suggestion thresholds differ). Rule (set by the integration lead after seeing how few items get a threshold at the engine default, but before this sweep was run): deploy the **lowest** target whose offered items on **validation** people have a synthetic false-recommendation rate (true benefit ≤ 0) ≤ 15% over at least 20 offered items; if none qualifies, keep the engine default 0.75. This is a **product trade-off between reach (how many people get a suggestion) and wrong suggestions**, decided on synthetic data.

## 13. Limitations

- **Synthetic only.** Every number above comes from a world we wrote (docs/ml-personalisation/v2/SYNTHETIC-DATA.md). A model that does well here has recovered structure we put in. Real people may relate answers, settings and benefit very differently, or not at all.
- The "true benefit" is our construct. Real benefit can only be estimated from behaviour (kept, undone, ratings), which is noisy and biased by what was shown.
- The logged evaluation inherits the logging policy's exposure bias (rules, popularity, a little exploration); no off-policy correction is applied.
- Fairness is measured on synthetic groups with a deliberately weak link between age band and traits. It says nothing about real disparities.
- See docs/ml-personalisation/v2/FAIRNESS.md and PIPELINE.md.
